Case 02 of 08 · Systems

Happy Money / HIVE / 2021–2023

Moving money was the easy part.

Happy Money had a capital problem hiding inside an operational one. Credit unions originated loans through Happy Money, but moving those loans between institutions through participation agreements was still largely manual. Every transaction worked. It just required a lot of people to keep it working.

Happy Money acquired Fiscal Hive to change that. I became the sole product designer responsible for turning that acquired technology into HIVE: an enterprise platform that could support buyers, sellers, and Happy Money operations through the full lifecycle of a loan participation.

At a glance

The problem
Loan participations relied on fragmented processes across spreadsheets, file transfers, email, third-party systems, and manual reconciliation.
The opportunity
Bring the participation lifecycle onto one platform while preserving the checks, transparency, and human judgment required to move money responsibly.
My role
Sole product designer across the buyer, seller, and Happy Money admin experiences. I partnered with engineering on information architecture and system behavior, gathered requirements, facilitated credit union partner roundtables, participated in UAT, and took on portions of the product-management work as the platform evolved.
The product
An enterprise loan-participation platform connecting pool creation, due diligence, fractional allocation, agreements, transaction management, and servicing data.
The principle
Automate the work. Make the rules visible. Preserve the decision.
RoleSole product designer
Timeframe2021–2023
TeamEng / Partners / Operations
PrincipleRules stayed visible

01 / The situation

Before there was HIVE, there was a spreadsheet.

A typical participation involved far more than transferring loans from one financial institution to another.

Happy Money’s operations teams had to identify eligible loans, construct portfolios, calculate ownership percentages, prepare loan tapes, reconcile fields, route documents, coordinate due diligence, track contracts, update core systems, and eventually produce remittance reporting. Much of that happened outside a dedicated product.

A spreadsheet might contain the transaction. An email explained what changed. Another system recorded ownership. A separate file documented settlement. And someone had to know how all of those pieces fit together.

The process depended heavily on institutional knowledge. That created more than inefficiency. It created risk. If the wrong loan entered a pool, an ownership percentage was calculated incorrectly, or a document was missed during due diligence, the problem wasn’t cosmetic. It could affect compliance, settlement, partner trust, and the integrity of the transaction itself.

HIVE needed to reduce operational friction without removing the controls that kept the process safe.

02 / The rules

The first challenge wasn’t design. It was understanding why the rules existed.

Most of my Happy Money work had focused on consumer lending experiences. HIVE dropped me into the institutional side of the business.

I had to learn how credit unions bought and sold fractional interests in loans, how participation agreements worked, why originating institutions retained ownership, how buyers evaluated a portfolio, how loan-level exceptions affected a pool, how servicing continued after the transaction, and where Happy Money sat between all of those parties.

Some complexity could be automated. Some complexity existed for a reason. That distinction became essential.

Retaining a required ownership percentage wasn’t simply another field in the interface. It represented a regulatory and contractual obligation. Due diligence wasn’t unnecessary friction. It was how buyers built confidence in the assets they were purchasing. Legal agreements weren’t a final administrative step. They were part of the trust infrastructure surrounding the transaction.

Before I could simplify the experience, I had to understand which safeguards needed to remain visible.

03 / Three jobs

One transaction. Three very different jobs.

The same pool of loans meant something different depending on who logged in.

Happy Money needed control.

Internal administrators needed to create and monitor transactions, understand exceptions, manage participating institutions, verify allocations, and make sure activity in HIVE remained synchronized with the underlying lending infrastructure.

Sellers needed clarity.

The originating credit union needed to know exactly which assets were being sold, what percentage it retained, what obligations remained, and where the transaction stood.

Buyers needed confidence.

The purchasing institution needed to understand what it was buying, inspect the portfolio, access supporting documentation, evaluate risk, flag exceptions, and approve a multimillion-dollar transaction without feeling like critical information was hidden behind automation.

The design challenge wasn’t to give each user a dashboard. It was to create a shared system where each party could understand the same transaction through the lens of their responsibility. That was as much a trust problem as an information-architecture problem.

04 / Project BEE

Project BEE gave us the first operating model.

The early phase of HIVE focused on getting the core participation flow onto the platform.

The workflow connected six major stages: pool creation, allocation, due diligence, agreement, transaction, and remittance.

Loans designated for participation could move from Happy Money’s core lending environment into HIVE. Ownership could be configured fractionally between institutions. Buyers could inspect the pool and review individual loans. Rejected loans could be removed before execution. Participation agreements could move through DocuSign. Completed transactions could update the loan’s underlying investor status. Servicing and remittance information could eventually flow back through the platform.

That gave us the skeleton. Partner feedback showed us what people needed in order to trust it.

05 / Due diligence

Due diligence wasn’t friction to remove. It was confidence we needed to design for.

One of the clearest lessons from credit union partners was that a participation did not begin with a loan tape. Before a buyer was prepared to purchase assets, its teams needed confidence in the institution, lending program, legal structure, and underlying portfolio.

Historically, much of that work happened through bilateral meetings, email threads, and secure file exchanges. We brought more of that process into HIVE. Selling institutions could provide legal, program, and asset documentation within the platform. Sensitive materials could remain protected behind NDA gating through DocuSign. Buyers could conduct more of their review without piecing together evidence from multiple channels.

The goal wasn’t to make due diligence disappear. It was to make it easier to understand what had been reviewed, what remained outstanding, and what the buyer was being asked to approve. In a high-trust financial product, reducing clicks means very little if users become less certain about what they’re agreeing to.

06 / Navigation

We couldn’t scale a product that needed a tour guide.

The acquired interface reflected the structure of the technology more than the way credit union users thought about their work. As long as only a few institutions used HIVE, Happy Money could compensate with training. That stopped being viable as the network grew.

I led the redesign of the navigation and page hierarchy around user intent rather than backend structure. Partners needed to know:

  • Where am I?
  • What am I responsible for?
  • What changed?
  • What needs my attention?
  • What happens if I take this action?

The objective wasn’t simply better navigation. It was reducing the amount of institutional knowledge required to use the product confidently. A credit union administrator shouldn’t need to understand Happy Money’s internal architecture to do their job.

07 / Automation

Automation changed what we designed.

As more of the workflow became connected to APIs and internal services, the backend grew substantially more sophisticated. The experience needed to move in the opposite direction.

Generating a pool might trigger data retrieval, field mapping, allocation logic, amortization calculations, and eligibility checks. Removing one loan during due diligence could affect the total pool balance, ownership percentages, compliance requirements, and downstream agreements. None of those calculations needed to become another spreadsheet exercise for the user.

Automate the work. Make the rules visible. Preserve the decision.

The system could assemble the pool. The user still needed to understand what was in it. The system could recalculate an allocation. The user still needed to see what changed and why. The system could detect an exception. The user still needed authority over what happened next. The system could enforce a compliance threshold. The interface still needed to explain the constraint rather than silently blocking the user.

Automation was not about removing professionals from the process. It was about removing repetitive labor while keeping judgment, accountability, and consequential decisions human.

08 / Compliance

Compliance became a design material.

In HIVE, some of the most important requirements weren’t visible features. They were rules.

Ownership thresholds. Loan eligibility requirements. Credit-box criteria. Document access. Legal approvals. Transaction states. Instead of treating those requirements as backend constraints, we had to decide how they should appear in the experience.

A compliance rule could become a warning before an invalid action. An eligibility requirement could become a visible exception rather than an unexplained error. A loan removed from the pool could trigger updated totals immediately so users understood the downstream effect. Sensitive documentation could remain inaccessible until the appropriate agreement was executed.

The system was enforcing policy. The interface needed to communicate that policy in a way that felt protective rather than punitive.

Compliance isn’t just something a product passes. It’s something users experience.

09 / The tape

A loan tape contained everything. The interface couldn’t.

The legacy workflow revolved around enormous spreadsheets. Thousands of loans could each carry dozens of fields: rate, APR, term, credit attributes, balances, payment information, servicing details, and underwriting characteristics.

Technically, all of that information mattered. Practically, showing all of it at once made it harder to make a decision. So I structured the experience around progressive disclosure. Portfolio-level metrics helped partners understand the overall pool. Risk indicators and filters helped them decide where deeper review was necessary. Loan-level attributes and documentation became available when investigation required more detail.

The data never disappeared. We changed when it demanded attention. That made the interface feel less like a database and more like a tool for decision-making.

10 / Feedback

Trust meant showing what the system changed.

A faster financial workflow is not automatically a better one. Automation can become uncomfortable when users cannot see what happened behind the scenes. That meant consequential system actions needed feedback.

If a buyer rejected a loan, the product could recalculate the pool automatically. But the updated pool balance needed to be visible. If ownership percentages changed, users needed to understand the new allocation. If a threshold was breached, the platform needed to explain the exception. If a transaction moved forward, users needed confidence that the underlying state had moved with it.

The goal wasn’t to expose every technical operation. It was to avoid the feeling that the product was making invisible financial decisions on the user’s behalf.

11 / Partners

Partner roundtables kept us close to the humans behind the institutions.

“Enterprise user” can become an abstraction quickly. In reality, HIVE was being used by people carrying significant responsibility inside financial institutions. They had risk committees to answer to. Audit requirements. Internal processes. Legal teams. Deadlines. And often years of experience working through spreadsheets and established operational routines.

I facilitated partner roundtables to put workflows and concepts directly in front of those users. Those conversations helped us understand not just whether someone could complete a task, but whether the product gave them enough confidence to change how they worked.

They influenced the platform materially. Counterparty due diligence moved into HIVE. Navigation was restructured around discoverability. The servicing model expanded. Batch-oriented workflows began shifting toward continuous data exchange. The product became less about what the acquired technology could do and more about what our partners needed to feel comfortable depending on it.

12 / What we left

Not every acquired feature deserved to survive.

Fiscal Hive came with a proprietary neural deep-learning model intended to forecast loan losses. It sounded like exactly the kind of technology an acquired fintech platform should preserve. But novelty wasn’t the same thing as trust.

Happy Money’s Science and Credit teams evaluated the model and found that it did not meaningfully outperform risk frameworks already understood by the market. Those existing frameworks also had an important advantage: credit union risk teams knew how to evaluate them.

Rather than asking partners to trust an unfamiliar black box, the team deprioritized the acquired model and leaned into recognized scoring and credit-box structures.

A sophisticated system isn’t useful if the people responsible for the decision don’t trust how it reached the answer.

13 / The ledger

The product’s role kept getting bigger.

Initially, HIVE could have remained a transaction facilitator. Create the participation. Complete the agreement. Hand the remaining work back to the institutions. But the difficult operational work didn’t end when the deal closed.

Ownership still had to be tracked. Servicing activity still had to be reconciled. Monthly remittance still had to be calculated. Portfolio allocations still needed to remain accurate. So the product evolved from broker toward ledger.

Instead of simply helping institutions complete a sale, HIVE could become a durable source of truth behind the relationship between the participating institutions and the loans they shared. That was a fundamentally different responsibility. Once partners began depending on HIVE as a source of truth, consistency and transparency were no longer just usability concerns. They were trust concerns.

14 / The shift

From spreadsheet workflow to decision interface.

Before HIVE, many participation activities required people to extract, calculate, compare, email, reconcile, recalculate, and confirm. The goal was to move more of that experience toward review, investigate, and decide.

Before

Extract, calculate, compare, email, reconcile, recalculate, confirm.

Toward

Review, investigate, decide.

The change wasn’t about removing humans. It was about respecting their attention. The system could perform repetitive calculations more reliably than a person moving between spreadsheets. The person could evaluate risk, investigate an exception, understand context, and make the decision. HIVE worked best when each did what it was better suited to do.

15 / The work

What I actually designed.

The visible output included buyer, seller, and administrative interfaces. The design work extended much further.

I helped define how users moved between portfolio-level and loan-level information. I worked with engineering to create an information architecture that reflected the transaction without exposing unnecessary system complexity. I designed workflows across pool creation, portfolio evaluation, loan review, documentation, transaction states, and servicing. I translated compliance rules into understandable product behavior.

I gathered requirements from internal teams. I facilitated credit union partner conversations to validate usability and trust. I participated in UAT. And because the product itself was still taking shape, I regularly operated beyond the traditional boundary of product design, helping translate business, operational, and regulatory requirements into product behavior.

16 / What it taught me

HIVE was my introduction to enterprise design.

And it fundamentally changed how I thought about complexity. Enterprise products aren’t complicated because designers haven’t simplified them enough. They’re complicated because the work itself carries dependencies, regulations, institutional responsibilities, and consequences.

Good enterprise design doesn’t pretend those things don’t exist. It decides which complexity belongs in the system, which needs to remain visible, and how much context a person needs to act confidently.

HIVE taught me that automation without transparency creates distrust. Compliance without explanation creates friction. And simplicity without context can become dangerous.

Let the system carry the burden of the process so people could focus on the decisions only they could make.

The closer

I wasn’t designing screens for a complicated financial process.

The trust layer

I was designing the trust layer between people, institutions, and the system moving their money.
