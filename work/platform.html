Case 01 of 08 · Platform migration

Happy Money / Platform migration / 2024–2025

The system had to change. The business couldn’t stop.

We had about nine months to replace a critical piece of Happy Money’s lending infrastructure. The deadline was fixed. The team was small. And while we rebuilt the foundation underneath the application, people still needed to apply for loans every day.

At a glance

The problem
A critical piece of the lending infrastructure had to be replaced on a fixed deadline, while new applications, existing applicants, support, and partners still depended on the experience.
Why it mattered
There was no version of the project where we could stop the funnel, rebuild quietly, and turn it back on when we were ready.
My role
Top-of-funnel product owner, lead experience designer, and UAT lead. I worked across product and design.
What I changed
What needed to change, what could stay, what had to wait, and how the technical shift would show up for customers.
The result
The new foundation cut over in January 2025. A small team replaced a critical part of the company without stopping the business that depended on it.
RoleProduct owner, design, UAT
Timeframe2024 → Jan 2025
TeamProduct / Design / Eng / Ops
OutcomeCut over, business kept moving

01 / The situation

Nine months. Small team. A lot on the line.

The legacy system was reaching the end of its life with us.

That gave us a hard deadline to move the lending experience onto a new foundation without disrupting originations. New applications still had to come in. Existing applicants still had to move forward. Internal teams still needed to support customers. Partners still depended on the experience.

This wasn’t a redesign with a clean starting point and a neat launch at the end. It was product, design, engineering, compliance, and operations working through dozens of dependencies at once while keeping the existing business moving.

02 / The tension

Every decision had to hold two clocks.

Where the product needed to go next, and what the business needed to keep working today.

A fixed date
About nine months. The deadline did not move.
A live business
Applicants, support, and partners could not pause while the foundation was replaced.
A small team
Scope had to be a product decision, not a list of everything that would be nice to finish.

03 / My role

The work lived in the spaces between teams.

Change the foundation without making customers carry the complexity.

Product owner

I helped define requirements, sequence the work, write stories, coordinate dependencies, and make scope calls.

Designer

I was responsible for how those decisions showed up for customers, often before there was anything to design.

A technical constraint becomes an experience state. An experience decision creates an engineering dependency. A timeline decision becomes a scope decision. A support concern becomes a routing rule.

04 / The moves

Protect the foundation first. Improve from there.

  1. Design for a system that doesn’t always answer right away

    The old experience assumed the system would return an answer immediately.

    The new foundation didn’t always work that way.

    I designed the waiting, checking, and fallback states that told people what was happening, when they could safely leave, and what would happen next.

    The goal was simple: the customer should never have to understand the architecture underneath the experience.

  2. Remove friction that wasn’t earning its place

    Applicants were being asked to create and remember a password in the middle of an already demanding financial process.

    We replaced password entry with one-time codes and removed password creation from offer selection.

    I later introduced Magic Link as another way back in. We built it, but kept it out of the main funnel because one-time codes were already working well there. It found a better fit in the co-branded sign-in experience.

    Not every new capability needs to be turned on everywhere.

  3. Keep internal handoffs from becoming customer dead ends

    Not every dependency in the new flow lived inside the customer-facing experience.

    Some steps needed to hand work off to internal tools or backend systems. I helped define the deep links and routing between those surfaces so teams could move from the customer context into the right operational tool without losing the thread.

    It was a small piece of the migration, but an important one: the experience had to make sense for the people supporting it too.

  4. Test the experience end to end

    As launch got closer, the question stopped being whether individual screens worked.

    It became whether a customer could actually make it through the whole journey.

    I joined the team onsite for end-to-end testing, moving through the funnel the way a customer would and documenting where system behavior, business rules, and the intended experience stopped lining up.

    We worked through those gaps with product and engineering in real time.

    Those were the moments most likely to become customer problems later.

What I chose not to doWhy

Stop the funnel and rebuild quietly

Applications, support, and partners still depended on the experience. The migration had to happen while the business kept moving.

Magic Link on the main funnel

One-time codes were already working. The capability shipped in the co-branded sign-in experience instead.

Historical documents in the first release

They would have widened the migration without protecting the journey we needed at launch. Teams who still needed them got an operational path.

05 / What changed

Company growth in the year following the cutover.

$4M → $14M Revenue

Company growth in the year following the cutover.

Automation More decisions, less manual review

Dynamic verification and income bypass gave the business new ways to move qualified applicants through without routing everyone into the same manual process.

Access Less password. More control.

One-time codes replaced password entry, password creation came out of offer selection, and Magic Link later gave returning users another way back in.

In hindsight

A small team replaced a critical part of the company’s foundation without stopping the business that depended on it.

Go deeper How the work was held together +

Necessary friction

Not every step is bad. The job is knowing which ones are doing useful work. One-time codes stayed because they were working. Magic Link was built, then held to the co-branded sign-in experience.

The gaps between systems

Some of the most important work happened onsite, in the small gaps where system behavior, business rules, and the intended experience stopped lining up. That is where a customer would eventually get stuck.

What this says about how I work

I’m most useful when the problem is bigger than the screen. This required design judgment, product judgment, technical understanding, and comfort with imperfect information. The work was helping a complicated system change without letting that complexity spill onto the people using it.
